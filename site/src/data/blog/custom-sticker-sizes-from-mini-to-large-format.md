Size is usually the last field filled in on an order form and the first thing
people regret. Too small and the detail that made the design work disappears;
too large and it will not sit flat on the surface it was made for. Both mistakes
are avoidable with two rules and a sheet of paper.

First, a note on how the number is measured. Sticker dimensions describe the
**bounding box**, the smallest rectangle that contains the shape, not the
widest part of the visible artwork. A 3-inch circular sticker is 3 inches across.
A 3-inch star is 3 inches at its widest point, so it looks distinctly smaller
than the circle even though both are quoted at 3 inches. Long, thin or irregular
shapes suffer most from this, and it is the single most common reason a delivered
sticker feels smaller than expected.

## Rule one: size for viewing distance

If the sticker carries text that has to be read, legibility sets the minimum
size. The working rule across the sign trade is **one inch of capital letter
height for every ten feet of viewing distance** for comfortable reading, or
roughly one inch per twenty feet for the absolute limit under good contrast.

Applied to real jobs:

- Read at arm's length, about 2 feet, 0.2 inch caps, so ordinary body copy is fine
- Read across a room, about 15 feet, 1.5 inch caps
- Read from a passing car, 50 feet plus, 5 inch caps minimum

Work backwards from the largest thing that must be readable, add the rest of the
design around it, and you have a minimum size rather than a guess. This is why
window graphics are usually far larger than people first assume, and why
[window stickers sized to the glass](/product/custom-window-stickers/) are
quoted from the reading distance on the pavement rather than the size of the
pane.

## Rule two: size for the finest detail

The other constraint comes from the bottom. Printing and cutting both have
practical limits, and shrinking artwork past them turns detail into mush.

- **Minimum line weight**, about 0.5 pt (0.18 mm) printed. Anything finer breaks up or disappears.
- **Minimum type size**, 6 pt for anything that must be read, 5 pt for legal small print at close range.
- **Minimum cut feature**, roughly 3 mm. Cut paths cannot resolve spikes, hairline gaps or zero-radius interior corners.
- **Minimum overall die cut piece**, around 0.5 inch. Below that, pieces are hard to peel and easy to lose.

Detailed illustration work, thin script logos and anything with an outline
around an outline needs size to survive. Bold marks with heavy strokes survive
almost any reduction, which is why the tiniest formats suit simple icons. Our
[small custom stickers for jars and mailers](/product/custom-small-stickers/)
work best with exactly that kind of simplified artwork.

The 2–4 inch band carries most of the volume in the industry, and for good
reason: it is large enough for a logo plus a short line of text, small enough to
fit almost anywhere, and cheap enough to hand out in quantity. If a size is not
obvious from the application, 3 inches is a defensible default.

## Real dimensions for common applications

- **Laptop lid**, 2 to 4 in. Anything larger competes with other stickers and the vent cutouts.
- **Water bottle or tumbler**, 2 to 3 in. Curved surfaces resist large flat shapes; keep the widest dimension under about 3 in or wrap it deliberately.
- **Candle or cosmetic jar lid**, measure the lid and subtract 1/4 in so nothing overhangs the rim.
- **Bottle body wrap**, circumference minus 1/8 in for the seam, height 40 to 60% of the straight wall section.
- **Mailer or box seal**, 1.5 to 2.5 in. Large enough to be seen, small enough not to tear the carton on opening.
- **Bumper**, the traditional 3 × 10 in still fits nearly every vehicle.
- **Rear windshield**, 4 to 8 in tall for a logo, or a full-width strip 4 to 6 in for lettering.
- **Vehicle door panel**, 12 to 24 in. This is a decal job with transfer tape, not a sticker job.
- **Hard hat**, 1.5 to 2 in, and it must curve without lifting.

For anything at the top of that list, a bigger piece needs a material that can
handle both the area and the application method, which is what
[large die cut stickers in custom shapes](/product/large-die-cut-stickers/) are
built for.

## How size affects what you pay

Cost does not scale with area the way people expect, because two different
things are being paid for: material and setup.

Setup is fixed per job. Artwork checks, cut path preparation and press
calibration cost the same whether you order fifty stickers or five thousand,
which is why unit price collapses as quantity rises and why very small orders
feel expensive per piece.

Material is where size enters, and it is charged by the area consumed on the
sheet or roll, including the gaps between pieces. Small stickers gang tightly , 
hundreds fit on one sheet, so the setup cost divides across all of them.
Doubling a sticker's width and height quadruples its area, and past a certain
point a piece stops ganging efficiently and starts consuming a whole sheet
width. That step is where prices jump, and it is worth asking a supplier where
it falls for your artwork before finalising dimensions.

Odd shapes cost more than compact ones at the same nominal size for the same
reason: a long diagonal shape wastes the material around it.
[Stickers grouped by shape and size](/product-category/shape-size/) is a useful
way to see which formats nest efficiently.

## Two checks before you approve

**Print it at 100% and tape it up.** Print the artwork at actual size on plain
paper, cut it out, and put it on the real laptop, jar or window. Thirty seconds
of this catches more sizing errors than any amount of on-screen zooming, because
a screen gives no reliable sense of physical scale.

**Read it from the real distance.** Stand where the customer will stand. If any
line of text is a struggle, the sticker is too small or the type is too fine , 
and enlarging the sticker is usually the cheaper of the two fixes.

Once the dimension is settled, the remaining decision is what the piece actually
is: a peel-and-place sticker, a decal that needs transfer tape, or a label
destined for an applicator. That distinction is covered in our guide to
[die cut stickers, decals and labels](/blog/die-cut-stickers-vs-decals-vs-labels/),
and it changes how a large order is supplied more than the size does.
