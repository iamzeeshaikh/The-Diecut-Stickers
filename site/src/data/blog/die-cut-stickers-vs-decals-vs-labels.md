Ask three suppliers what a decal is and you will get three answers, at least one
of which is "the same as a sticker." That is not entirely wrong, the words
overlap heavily in everyday use, but the products behind them are ordered,
supplied and applied differently enough that using the wrong term will get you
the wrong quote. The distinction that actually matters is not the name. It is
how the piece is cut, how it arrives, and how it goes on.

## What "die cut" means

Die cutting is cutting the material to a shape other than a rectangle. The name
comes from the steel rule die, a shaped blade mounted in a press that stamps
each piece out. Most short and medium runs today are cut digitally instead, by a
blade or laser following a vector path from your artwork, which removes the
tooling cost and makes small quantities of complex shapes practical.

There are two depths of cut, and confusing them is the single most common
ordering mistake.

**Kiss cut** slices through the printed face and the adhesive but stops at the
liner beneath. The backing stays whole, so the sticker sits on a square or
rectangular carrier and peels away from it cleanly. This is the format for
sheets, packs and anything a customer will peel themselves.

**Through cut**, which most people mean when they say "die cut," goes through
face, adhesive and liner together. The result is a single piece shaped exactly
like the artwork with no surrounding border at all, the format that makes a
logo look like a logo rather than a rectangle with a logo printed on it. Our
[logo stickers cut to your outline](/product/die-cut-logo-stickers/) are
produced this way, which is why they read as part of the surface they are
applied to.

## Sticker

A sticker is the consumer-facing product: a single printed piece with adhesive
on the back, usually decorative or promotional, applied by hand by whoever
receives it. Laptop stickers, packaging seals, giveaway sheets, merch packs.

Stickers are typically supplied as singles or on sheets, printed on paper or
vinyl, and they are applied in one motion with no tooling. The defining
characteristic is that the printed design and the adhesive are one piece, you
peel it, you place it, you are done. Most orders in this category use
[die cut vinyl sticker stock](/product/die-cut-vinyl-stickers/) because vinyl
handles curved surfaces, water and everyday abuse better than paper.

## Decal

A decal is generally larger, more durable, and intended for a hard surface that
gets weather or wear: vehicles, windows, equipment, storefronts, signage. The
practical difference is often application method rather than material.

Anything beyond a simple single shape needs **transfer tape**, a low-tack
carrier film laid over the face that holds every piece in position while the
liner is removed, so lettering and multi-part artwork transfer as one aligned
unit instead of thirty loose pieces. That three-layer sandwich, liner plus decal
plus transfer tape, is what most people in the trade actually mean by "decal."
Our [car decals applied with transfer tape](/product/die-cut-car-stickers/) ship
this way as standard, with application instructions, because a fleet marking
applied one letter at a time never lines up.

Decals also cover **cut vinyl**, where there is no printing at all: the design
is cut from a solid colored film, and everything that is not part of the design
is weeded away by hand before transfer tape goes on. Window lettering and
storefront hours are usually cut vinyl. It has no printed background, so the
surface shows through every gap in the design, the reason it looks painted on.

### Weeding and detail limits

Weeding sets the detail limit. Very thin strokes, tight interior counters and
serif details become impossible to remove cleanly at small sizes. If a design
has fine detail and must be small, print it rather than cut it. Broader
weather-durability differences between materials are covered in our guide to
[how long outdoor stickers last](/blog/how-long-do-outdoor-stickers-last/).

## Label

A label is functional. It carries information the product needs to carry , 
brand, contents, weight, ingredients, barcode, batch code, compliance marks , 
and it is applied to a product or its packaging, usually as part of production
rather than by the end customer.

The format difference is the real one. Labels are commonly supplied on rolls
rather than sheets, because roll stock feeds automated applicators. If you are
using a labelling machine, the order needs three extra specifications a sticker
order never mentions: **roll diameter**, **core size** (usually 1 or 3 inch),
and **unwind direction**, which of eight orientations the label leaves the roll
in. Getting unwind wrong means a correct print that no machine can apply.

Labels also inherit regulatory requirements. Food, cosmetic, supplement and
pharmaceutical labelling all carry rules on what must appear, at what minimum
type size, and how permanent it has to be. Barcodes need a quiet zone and enough
contrast to scan, which quietly rules out some finishes and colored stocks.
[Custom packaging labels with barcodes](/product/custom-packaging-labels/) are
specified with adhesive and facestock chosen so the carton underneath stays
recyclable, which matters more than it used to.

## Preparing artwork for any of the three

The requirements are the same regardless of what you call the product.

- **Supply vector artwork**, PDF, AI or EPS. Raster files cannot carry a cut path.
- **Include a dieline** on its own layer, as a single closed vector path in a named spot color. Do not merge it into the artwork.
- **Bleed 1/8 inch** past the cut line, so a small registration shift never leaves a white sliver at the edge.
- **Offset the cut path** slightly outward from the design rather than tracing it exactly. A 2–3 mm border is standard and makes the shape far more robust.
- **Keep critical content 1/8 inch inside** the cut line, the same safe margin used in print generally.
- **Simplify tight interior corners.** A cutting blade cannot turn a zero-radius corner; a small radius cuts cleanly and does not tear.
